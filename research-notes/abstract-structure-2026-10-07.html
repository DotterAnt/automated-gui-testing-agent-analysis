What these examples suggest for your abstract

The recurring argument is why the task matters → what remains difficult → what this work contributes → how it was evaluated → what changed → what the finding means. The ordering is flexible: AlphaCode combines its system and headline result; FunSearch returns to the mechanism after presenting discoveries. These are patterns observed in this selection, not mandatory journal rules.

For your thesis, I would use a six-to-eight-sentence progression. This is a planning scaffold, not a draft abstract:

  1. Problem. State the practical difficulty of producing reliable GUI tests for desktop applications.
  2. Gap. Identify the specific limitation your framework addresses. Avoid a sweeping claim that GUI-test automation does not exist.
  3. Contribution. Introduce the framework and the exploration, script-generation and validation workflow it enables.
  4. Evaluation. Name the comparison groups and tested applications. Define which experiments belong to the main comparison and which are model-version extensions.
  5. Main result. Give one clear quantitative effect for authoring time, with its baseline and aggregation method.
  6. Outcome and boundary. Add the relevant completion or validation outcome and a material failure pattern. Partial or failed attempts should shape the interpretation.
  7. Meaning. Explain what the findings support within the evaluated tasks and applications.

Keep authoring/session duration separate from generated-script execution time. State what counts as success, and distinguish a script's reported validation result from evidence that it followed the required GUI route. Make model-version claims only where the tasks and applications are comparable. These are recommendations based on your experiment design.

Useful balances to emulate: DroidAgent combines autonomy, coverage and task relevance; the coordination study combines gains with overhead and a scope qualification; the diffusion study integrates a null subgroup result into its conclusion.
